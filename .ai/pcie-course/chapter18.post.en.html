The previous chapter separated credit, Ack, and transaction completion. Here a TLP (Transaction Layer Packet) enters a Flit (fixed-size transfer unit) and crosses the Lanes to the receiver. First follow normal placement and checks, then use Selective Replay to see why TLP content and DLP are handled differently.

Choose the mode before the wire decoder

64.0 GT/s uses PAM4 (four-level pulse-amplitude modulation) and Flit Mode. Lower rates can also use Flit Mode. Establish the Link, direction, rate, and mode at the observation time before decoding a trace.

The identification note on p.309 distinguishes Flit Mode Supported in the PCI Express Capabilities Register, Flit Mode Disable in Link Control, and Flit Mode Status in Link Status 2. The last field describes operation in Flit Mode now or in the future. Training advertisements and the successfully negotiated variable Flit_Mode_Enabled are separate information.

Follow normal correction and checking first

PAM4 (two bits per interval using four voltage levels) increases signaling density, but smaller eye height and width increase susceptibility to errors. FEC (Forward Error Correction) attempts correction before the Flit CRC check.

Read Figures 4-22 and 4-23 together. The transmitter generates eight CRC bytes from the 236-byte TLP area and six-byte DLP, generates ECC, then distributes the Flit across Lanes. The receiver performs applicable Lane decoding and descrambling, aggregates the Flit, applies FEC decoding/correction, and checks CRC. These are conceptual processing diagrams, not a mandate for identical internal datapath widths.

For the x4 case, assume one correctable byte error is corrected, no ECC group reports an uncorrectable error, CRC passes, and Flit Usage and Flit_Status use legal encodings. This meets the valid-Flit conditions in §4.2.3.4.2. Sequence, replay state, and TLP-format rules still determine which TLP content can be delivered; a passing CRC does not complete a Request.

The three colors in Table 4-12 identify ECC groups, not Lanes. Each group can correct one byte error. The distribution across groups matters, so a total count of erroneous bytes alone is insufficient. A CRC failure or an ECC-group uncorrectable indication prevents valid-Flit treatment; receive and replay rules then apply.

Must a replayed Flit be byte-for-byte identical?

TLP content is replayed; DLP carries current management information. Section 4.2.3.3 excludes DLP from the Link Layer Retry Buffer. Since CRC covers DLP, CRC and ECC are generated for the content being transmitted now.

Decode the reverse-direction Selective Nak

CRC still fails after FEC processing at the Endpoint. Assume later received information identifies the invalid Flit as Payload, sufficient RX Retry Buffer exists, and the Endpoint chooses Selective Nak (a request to replay one specified Flit). One valid reverse-direction NOP Flit carries 1D 29 80 00 00 00. Its preceding Flit in that direction was also NOP, so Prior Flit was Payload is zero.

Here Replay Command=11b. Sequence 129h means the last valid received Flit, and the requested replay is 129h + 1 = 12Ah. It refers to Root Port → Endpoint traffic, not a newly assigned sequence for this reverse NOP Flit. Command 10b would mean Standard Nak, requesting all unacknowledged Flits rather than this single-Flit scope.

Compare replay content and the receive outcome

The Root Port schedules replay of 12Ah, retrieving TLP content from its TX Retry Buffer and using current DLP information. When it sends the replay with an Explicit Sequence Number, it uses the stored 12Ah. The Endpoint applies validity and sequence checks and processes stored/replayed content in receive order, preventing replay from becoming a newly delivered transaction.
